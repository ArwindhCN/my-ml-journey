# Chapter 10.2 — Batch Normalisation

> This notebook assumes Chapter 9 (Neural Networks) and Chapter 10.1 (Regularisation — Dropout, L2, Early Stopping). The running analogy is **Ravi, a loan officer at a Chennai bank**, with junior officers (hidden neurons) reporting up to a senior officer (the next layer).

---

## 1. The Problem — Why a Stable Input Isn't Enough

Back in Chapter 9/2, you learned to **scale the inputs** before training (`StandardScaler`, fit on train only) — because raw features like income (₹15,000–₹500,000) and age (18–70) sit on wildly different scales, and that scale mismatch destabilizes gradient descent.

But that scaling happens **once**, right at the very start, before data ever enters the network. Once inside a deep network, the *output* of layer 1 (call it `h₁`) becomes the *input* to layer 2. And `h₁` is not a fixed, pre-scaled quantity — it's computed live, every forward pass, from layer 1's current weights:

`h₁ = ReLU(w₁·x + b₁)`

Every single gradient descent step updates `w₁` and `b₁` — even by a small amount. Since `h₁` is a direct function of those weights, **the distribution of `h₁`'s values shifts every training step**, purely as a side effect of layer 1 learning — even though the raw input `x` never changes. Early in training `h₁` might average around 0.5 with a small spread. A hundred steps later, after `w₁` has moved, `h₁` might average around 3.2 with a much wider spread. Nobody told it to move — it's a side effect of the layer beneath it changing.

This has a name: **internal covariate shift** — the input distribution to any given layer keeps drifting internally, purely because earlier layers keep changing.

### 1.1 Why This Hurts the Next Layer

Layer 2's weights (`w₂`) are tuned by gradient descent *under the assumption* that its inputs (`h₁`) come from roughly the same distribution as the last step. If `h₁`'s scale keeps moving, layer 2 is chasing a **moving target** — it has to spend gradient updates just re-adjusting to the new *scale* of its inputs, instead of purely learning the actual pattern.

**Ravi analogy:** the senior officer is told "junior 1's reports used to range 0–1, now suddenly they range 0–10" every few days. He has to relearn what "normal" looks like on top of learning the actual lending decision. Training slows down badly, and you're forced into tiny learning rates just to avoid the whole thing becoming unstable.

There's a second, sneakier effect: if `h₁`'s values drift to become very large or very negative, and the next layer uses **Sigmoid or Tanh**, those large values push into the flat, saturated regions of the curve (Chapter 9.3) — killing the gradient (vanishing gradient). Drifting activations don't just slow training; they can actively choke it.

---

## 2. The Fix — Force the Distribution to Stay Stable

Instead of hoping `h₁`'s distribution stays put, **force it to stay stable, at every layer, on every training step.**

### 2.1 Where the Mean and Variance Actually Come From

This is the detail that's easy to get backwards, so it's worth being precise. Training never processes one applicant at a time — it processes a **mini-batch** (say, 32 applicants) together, in one forward pass. That means the *same* neuron — the one computing `h₁ = ReLU(w₁x + b₁)` — produces **one separate value per applicant in the batch**, not one shared value for the whole batch:

```
Applicant 1  → h₁ = 0.42
Applicant 2  → h₁ = 1.87
Applicant 3  → h₁ = -0.15
...
Applicant 32 → h₁ = 0.93
```

You can't compute a mean or a spread from a single number — `μ_B` and `σ²_B` come from looking across these 32 different values, all produced by the same neuron, on the same training step:

```
μ_B  = mean(0.42, 1.87, -0.15, ..., 0.93)     # mean across the batch
σ²_B = variance(0.42, 1.87, -0.15, ..., 0.93) # variance across the batch
```

### 2.2 Normalizing — Individually, Not Merged

A common misconception here: the junior does **not** combine the 32 applicants into a single output. `μ_B` and `σ²_B` are only calculated to serve as a **shared yardstick**. Each applicant's own value is then normalized **individually** against that yardstick:

```
ĥ₁(applicant 1)  = (0.42 − μ_B) / √(σ²_B + ε)
ĥ₁(applicant 2)  = (1.87 − μ_B) / √(σ²_B + ε)
...
ĥ₁(applicant 32) = (0.93 − μ_B) / √(σ²_B + ε)
```

`ε` (like 1e-8) is a tiny constant that just prevents division by zero.

The senior officer (layer 2) still receives **32 separate inputs, one per applicant** — exactly like before BatchNorm existed — just each one is now rescaled against the batch's own mean and variance.

**Ravi analogy:** the junior officer briefly glances at all 32 applicants' raw scores just to calibrate "what's typical today" (that's `μ_B`, `σ²_B`) — like getting a sense of scale for the day. But he still writes 32 separate individual reports for the senior, one per applicant, each one adjusted against that day's typical scale. He never merges 32 people into one combined verdict — the senior still needs to make 32 separate loan decisions.

**Direct callback to Chapter 2:** this is conceptually identical to `StandardScaler.fit_transform()` — compute one mean/std from a set of values, then apply that same mean/std to transform *every row individually*, without ever merging the rows together. BatchNorm is doing that same operation, per neuron, per batch, mid-network, on every single training step — instead of once at the very start on the raw input.

### 2.3 The Learnable Scale and Shift — `γ` and `β`

The normalized value doesn't get used as-is. It's immediately passed through a learnable rescale and reshift:

```
y = γ·ĥ₁ + β
```

At first glance this looks self-defeating — why force `mean 0, variance 1` and then immediately hand the network a knob to undo it? Because forcing every layer's output to strictly mean-0/variance-1 is a *human-imposed* constraint, not necessarily what's mathematically optimal for that specific layer. For example, if that layer feeds into a Sigmoid, the network might want a slightly wider spread to use more of the sigmoid's active range. `γ` and `β` let the network **learn the best scale and shift for itself**, while still keeping the *stability* benefit — because `γ` and `β` are updated smoothly and slowly via gradient descent, not left to drift chaotically the way `h`'s raw scale would without normalization.

> **Normalization removes uncontrolled, chaotic drift. `γ`/`β` reintroduce controlled, intentional flexibility.** If strict normalization turns out to be exactly right for a given layer, gradient descent will simply learn `γ≈1, β≈0` and leave it alone.

## 3. Train vs. Test — A Critical Behavioral Difference

At training time, `μ_B` and `σ²_B` are computed **fresh from each mini-batch**. But at test time, you might be predicting for a **single applicant** — there's no "batch" to compute a mean/variance from. Even if there were, predictions shouldn't depend on which other examples happen to be in the same batch (Ravi's decision on you shouldn't depend on who else applied on the same day).

**Fix:** during training, BatchNorm also maintains a **running average** of `μ_B` and `σ²_B` across every batch it has seen (`running_mean`, `running_var`). At test time, it uses these fixed, accumulated statistics instead of computing anything from the current input.

```python
model.train()   # BatchNorm uses the CURRENT batch's mean/variance, and updates the running stats
model.eval()    # BatchNorm uses the stored running_mean / running_var instead
```

This is the exact same `.train()` / `.eval()` switch already seen with Dropout in 10.1 — both layers behave differently at train vs. test time, and forgetting `.eval()` breaks both in similar ways (unstable or inconsistent predictions).

## 4. Where It Goes — Before or After the Activation

Standard convention: **Linear/Dense layer → BatchNorm → Activation.**

```python
import torch.nn as nn

model = nn.Sequential(
    nn.Linear(5, 100),
    nn.BatchNorm1d(100),   # normalize BEFORE the activation
    nn.ReLU(),
    nn.Linear(100, 1),
    nn.Sigmoid()
)
```

Keras equivalent:

```python
from tensorflow.keras.models import Sequential
from tensorflow.keras.layers import Dense, BatchNormalization, Activation

model = Sequential([
    Dense(100, input_shape=(5,)),
    BatchNormalization(),
    Activation('relu'),
    Dense(1, activation='sigmoid')
])
```

There is some real debate in practice about before-vs-after activation — the original paper places it before; many practitioners place it after and it often works fine too. Before-activation is the safe default.

### 4.1 Combining With Dropout (10.1)

Order matters. Common convention:

```
Linear → BatchNorm → Activation → Dropout → next Linear
```

BatchNorm stabilizes the distribution first; Dropout then randomly silences neurons from that already-stabilized signal.

## 5. Code — BatchNorm in Context

```python
import torch.nn as nn

class LoanNet(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, input_dim):
        super().__init__()
        self.layer1 = nn.Linear(input_dim, 32)
        self.bn1 = nn.BatchNorm1d(32)
        self.dropout1 = nn.Dropout(0.3)

        self.layer2 = nn.Linear(32, 16)
        self.bn2 = nn.BatchNorm1d(16)
        self.dropout2 = nn.Dropout(0.3)

        self.layer3 = nn.Linear(16, 1)

        self.relu = nn.ReLU()
        self.sigmoid = nn.Sigmoid()

    def forward(self, x):
        x = self.relu(self.bn1(self.layer1(x)))
        x = self.dropout1(x)
        x = self.relu(self.bn2(self.layer2(x)))
        x = self.dropout2(x)
        x = self.sigmoid(self.layer3(x))
        return x
```

`model.train()` / `model.eval()` switches Dropout and BatchNorm's behaviour together, in one call — both layers respond correctly.

## 6. Merits and Demerits

**Merits:**
- Allows much **higher learning rates** — training doesn't destabilize as easily since scale is controlled at every layer
- Speeds up convergence significantly — fewer epochs typically needed
- Has a **mild regularization side-effect**: since batch statistics (`μ_B`, `σ²_B`) vary slightly from batch to batch (different random samples each time), it injects a small amount of noise — similar in spirit to Dropout — and can sometimes reduce (though never replace) the need for Dropout
- Reduces sensitivity to weight initialization choices

**Demerits:**
- **Batch-size dependent** — with very small batches (e.g. 2–4 examples), `μ_B`/`σ²_B` are noisy, unreliable estimates, and BatchNorm can hurt more than help. This matters directly for GNNs trained on small graphs/subgraphs.
- Adds extra learnable parameters (`γ`, `β` per neuron) and computation per layer
- Behaves differently in train vs. test — a common source of subtle bugs if `.eval()`/`.train()` mode is mismatched, exactly like Dropout
- Doesn't play as cleanly with certain architectures (RNNs historically struggled with it — alternatives like **LayerNorm** exist for those cases, reappearing in Chapter 13 with Transformers)

## 7. How BatchNorm Fits Alongside 10.1's Tools

| | Dropout | L2 | BatchNorm |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixes | Co-adaptation / overfitting | Large weights / overfitting | Internal covariate shift / unstable training |
| Train vs. test differ? | Yes | No | Yes |
| Adds learnable params? | No | No | Yes (`γ`, `β` per neuron) |
| Side-effect | — | — | Mild regularization |
| Batch-size sensitive? | No | No | Yes |

All three attack the same underlying disease (overfitting or unstable training, established in 10.1 Section 1) from different angles, and are typically combined rather than chosen between.
